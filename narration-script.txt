c01s03 Chapter 1: Different why questions can complement each other.
Proximate explanation: account of mechanisms and development. Ultimate explanation: account concerning evolutionary history or function. Level of explanation: the kind of question an account addresses.

A present motive, a learning history and an evolutionary hypothesis can concern the same behaviour without being interchangeable.

A person may help from genuine concern regardless of a proposed evolutionary history. Explaining a possible history does not reveal the person’s conscious purpose.

c01s03b Chapter 1: Tinbergen distinguished four questions.
Ontogeny: development within an individual lifetime. Phylogeny: evolutionary history across lineages.

Tinbergen distinguished mechanism, development, evolutionary history and adaptive function. A complete research programme can investigate several questions with different methods.

Do not substitute one answer for the others. Learning a practice during life does not rule out biological influences, and a biological account does not remove culture.

c01s04 Chapter 1: A function claim needs testing.
Adaptive function: proposed contribution to reproductive success in an evolutionary context. Alternative explanation: another account fitting some observations.

A useful-looking action is not automatically an adaptation. A function hypothesis must specify a problem, predictions and alternatives.

The café example is not evidence that table lifting evolved for one purpose. It can help formulate questions about learning, relationships and possible histories.

c01s05 Chapter 1: What does the evidence allow?
Evolution is an established biological framework. Specific claims about psychological adaptations vary in evidential support and require their own tests.

Tinbergen’s questions organize inquiry; naming a level of explanation does not prove the answer proposed at that level.

c02s03 Chapter 2: Selection changes populations over generations.
Variation: differences among individuals. Heritable variation: variation partly transmitted across generations. Natural selection: change associated with differential reproduction of heritable variants.

When heritable differences affect reproductive outcomes under particular conditions, their frequencies can change across generations. Selection has no foresight or intention.

Individuals develop and learn during life; populations evolve across generations. A useful habit acquired by one person is not automatically a genetic evolutionary change.

c02s03b Chapter 2: Fitness is not health, virtue or happiness.
Evolutionary fitness: relative contribution to subsequent generations in a context. Trade-off: benefit in one respect with a cost in another. Constraint: limitation on possible evolutionary change.

Fitness is defined relative to reproductive contribution under conditions. It does not measure moral worth or guarantee happiness, health or an optimal design.

Selection works with inherited structures and competing demands. A feature can have costs and still persist; some features persist for reasons other than selection.

c02s04 Chapter 2: Change has more than one source.
Genetic drift: random change in variant frequencies.

Evolutionary change can involve selection, drift, mutation and gene flow. Developmental constraints and historical contingencies also matter.

The observation that a feature exists today does not identify which process produced it. An adaptation claim needs more than a statement that the feature seems useful.

c02s05 Chapter 2: What does the evidence allow?
Natural selection is a general mechanism supported by converging biological evidence. A particular behavioural adaptation claim requires additional evidence about variation, inheritance, history and function.

A modern preference or group difference does not identify its evolutionary origin by itself.

c03s03 Chapter 3: Several origins can fit a present feature.
Adaptation: feature shaped by selection for a particular effect. Exaptation: feature used for a role different from an earlier origin.

A present benefit does not establish the historical cause of a feature. A capacity can be recruited for a new use, or emerge as a by-product.

These hypotheses can overlap in a complex trait. Music is a debated research topic, not an example whose origin the café anecdote can settle.

c03s03b Chapter 3: Design claims need discriminating predictions.
Special design: features fitting a proposed adaptive problem. Comparative evidence: comparisons across species or populations. Falsifiability: possibility of evidence counting against a claim.

An adaptation hypothesis should predict a pattern that alternatives do not equally predict. Anatomy, development, comparative work and other evidence can contribute.

If every possible outcome is explained after the fact, the account is difficult to test. Specify what would reduce confidence before inspecting only supportive cases.

c03s04 Chapter 3: Mismatch needs a specified contrast.
Evolutionary mismatch: a feature’s effects differ between relevant ancestral and current conditions. Current function: present effects under present conditions.

Mismatch hypotheses compare a specified feature and environmental change. There was no single uniform ancestral environment or one universally natural lifestyle.

A plausible mismatch does not prescribe a diet, relationship or social policy. Clinical and practical recommendations need their own outcome evidence.

c03s05 Chapter 3: What does the evidence allow?
Adaptation, by-product and mismatch hypotheses need independent evidence. Scholarly debate concerns how to distinguish them, not whether evolution occurred.

The music case illustrates alternative explanations. No one origin claim is established by enjoyment or prevalence alone.

c04s03 Chapter 4: Development constructs the phenotype.
Phenotype: an observed characteristic. Developmental system: interacting biological and environmental processes. Plasticity: capacity for change across relevant conditions.

Genes, cells, bodies and experience interact throughout development. An evolved capacity can depend on learning and culturally structured input.

The question is not which influence wins. Specify the developmental process and how it responds to different inputs and opportunities.

c04s03b Chapter 4: Heritability is a population statement.
Reaction norm: pattern of outcomes across environments for a genotype. Gene–environment interaction: genetic effects differing across environments.

Heritability depends on the population, environmental range and measure. It does not say what percentage of one person’s behaviour is genetic.

A trait can be heritable and responsive to changed conditions. Conversely, a widely shared developmental mechanism may generate little measured variation in one sample.

c04s04 Chapter 4: Learning can be part of an evolved solution.
Preparedness: differential ease of learning some relations under conditions. Learning bias: systematic tendency in acquiring information.

Learning is a biological capability expressed in environments. Research can test whether some associations are acquired differently under controlled conditions.

Such findings do not establish that every current fear or preference is innate. Prior experience, measurement and cultural input remain alternatives to examine.

c04s05 Chapter 4: What does the evidence allow?
Evolved and learned are not mutually exclusive categories. Developmental and genetic evidence must be interpreted at their appropriate levels.

Heritability does not establish immutability, adaptation or the cause of a specific person’s behaviour.

c05s03 Chapter 5: Fitness consequences can extend through relatives.
Kin selection: selection influenced by effects on relatives’ reproductive outcomes. Inclusive fitness: framework including direct and relatedness-weighted effects. Relatedness: genetic association relevant to an evolutionary model.

An action affecting relatives can influence the propagation of shared genetic variants. Kin-selection models formalize these effects under stated assumptions.

They do not claim that people consciously count genes or care only about relatives. Present motives and historical fitness consequences remain different questions.

c05s03b Chapter 5: Hamilton’s rule is conditional.
Hamilton’s rule: condition comparing relatedness-weighted benefit with cost. Fitness cost: reduction in reproductive outcome within a model. Fitness benefit: increase in reproductive outcome within a model.

The familiar rule compares relatedness times benefit with cost under a specified model. Cost and benefit concern fitness, not simply money or felt effort.

Real applications need appropriate definitions and evidence. A casual act of family help does not measure the terms or prove the model caused it.

c05s04 Chapter 5: Non-kin care is not a contradiction.
Direct benefit: effect on the actor’s own reproductive outcome in a model. Social norm: shared expectation influencing conduct.

Care can involve attachment, norms, reciprocity, institutions and personal values. Several evolutionary and proximate processes may contribute.

The existence of care for non-relatives does not by itself refute kin selection, nor does kin selection explain every act of care. Ethical obligations need independent reasoning.

c05s05 Chapter 5: What does the evidence allow?
Kin selection is a formal evolutionary framework with extensive biological applications. Applying it to a particular human act requires model assumptions and relevant evidence.

Relatedness does not determine whom someone should help, and a fitness account does not prove selfish conscious motivation.

c06s03 Chapter 6: Repeated interaction can change incentives.
Direct reciprocity: cooperation influenced by repeated interaction between partners. One-shot interaction: interaction modelled without future encounters.

Helping now can affect future treatment when individuals interact again. Models depend on memory, opportunities and costs.

Not all cooperation is delayed repayment. Mutual benefit, genuine concern and social rules can support action without one simple exchange account.

c06s03b Chapter 6: Reputation can affect a wider network.
Indirect reciprocity: help influenced by information about others’ social behaviour. Partner choice: selecting whom to interact with.

People can use reputation when choosing partners or helping. Information can be incomplete, mistaken or shaped by unequal visibility.

A public act may affect reputation without proving that reputation was the actor’s conscious goal. Compare private and public conditions carefully before inferring motives.

c06s04 Chapter 6: Institutions change the available relations.
Institution: shared rules and practices structuring interaction. Public good: benefit difficult to restrict to contributors. Coordination: aligning actions toward a compatible outcome.

Rules, trust, accountability and fair access can support cooperation. A shared checkout system changes the interaction rather than merely changing individual generosity.

Economic games isolate selected incentives, but their results depend on instructions, norms and sampling. A game score is not a complete measure of a person’s morality.

c06s05 Chapter 6: What does the evidence allow?
Reciprocity models generate conditional predictions. Human cooperation also involves development, norms and institutions, and experimental games simplify these conditions.

A modern game result does not establish one ancestral origin or reveal a hidden selfish intention.

c07s03 Chapter 7: Sexual selection concerns reproductive differences.
Sexual selection: selection associated with differences in mating or fertilization success. Mate choice: preferences or decisions affecting mating. Competition: interaction over limited opportunities or resources.

Sexual-selection models examine variation in reproductive outcomes under particular conditions. They apply across diverse organisms and mating systems.

Human relationships also involve affection, learning, institutions and individual identity. A reproductive model does not capture every form or purpose of a relationship.

c07s03b Chapter 7: Investment and constraints vary.
Parental investment: resources benefiting offspring at a cost to other reproductive opportunities. Life-history trade-off: allocation among growth, maintenance and reproduction. Ecological condition: environmental resource or risk affecting outcomes.

Models examine how costs, resources and opportunities affect reproductive strategies. Constraints and care arrangements vary across species and human societies.

Do not turn a model average into a fixed claim about every woman, man or family. Sex, gender, sexuality, resources and social roles require careful distinction in human research.

c07s04 Chapter 7: A difference needs a well-designed comparison.
Within-group variation: differences among members of a group. Effect size: magnitude of a measured difference or relation. Measurement equivalence: evidence that a measure has comparable meaning across groups.

Report the magnitude, overlap and uncertainty of a difference, and check sampling and cultural context. A group average is not an individual prediction.

A descriptive evolutionary hypothesis does not justify coercion, discrimination or a prescribed relationship arrangement. Ethics and practical decisions need independent reasons.

c07s05 Chapter 7: What does the evidence allow?
Sexual-selection and life-history models are established frameworks; particular human preference hypotheses have varying support and active debate. Cross-cultural evidence and alternative accounts matter.

This lesson provides neither dating advice nor a normative model for family life.

c08s03 Chapter 8: Information can spread socially.
Cultural transmission: socially mediated acquisition of information or practice. Social learning: learning influenced by observing or interacting with others.

A technique can spread through teaching, observation or imitation without a change in gene frequencies. Variants can change as people modify and combine them.

Cultural evolution studies these transmission processes and population patterns. Spread alone does not show that a practice is useful or fair.

c08s03b Chapter 8: Copying can follow different cues.
Conformist transmission: disproportionate copying of a common variant under a model. Prestige-biased transmission: copying influenced by a model’s social standing. Content bias: transmission influenced by features of the information.

People may attend to common practices, respected models or memorable content. These are hypotheses about transmission that need appropriate comparison.

A popular method may be effective, fashionable or simply more visible. Test actual outcomes rather than equating prevalence with quality.

c08s04 Chapter 8: Genes and culture can influence each other.
Gene–culture coevolution: interacting genetic and cultural changes over generations. Niche construction: organisms modify environments affecting subsequent development or selection. Cumulative culture: building on earlier socially transmitted contributions.

Social practices can change environments and opportunities, while biological capacities support learning and participation. These interactions can unfold over different timescales.

Do not infer a genetic change from rapid cultural spread. A coevolution claim needs historical and biological evidence beyond a modern trend.

c08s05 Chapter 8: What does the evidence allow?
Cultural-evolution models formalize transmission and change, but particular applications depend on assumptions and data. Cultural variation can also test claims about universality.

A popular practice is not automatically adaptive, optimal or ethically desirable.

c09s03 Chapter 9: Sampling limits the conclusion.
WEIRD sampling: sampling concentrated in Western, educated, industrialized, rich and democratic settings. Generalizability: applicability beyond studied conditions. Cross-cultural comparison: comparison across culturally situated populations.

A convenient sample may differ from much of the world in institutions, education and experience. A universal claim needs evidence beyond one narrow setting.

Cross-cultural work also needs comparable measures and local understanding. Translating words alone does not guarantee the same psychological construct was measured.

c09s03b Chapter 9: State a risky prediction in advance.
Competing prediction: outcome distinguishing alternative accounts.

A strong hypothesis states what pattern should occur and what would count against it. Compare adaptation, learning, institutional and by-product accounts where relevant.

Preregistration can reduce selective storytelling but does not fix a poor measure. Replication and converging designs strengthen a claim when the tests address its actual prediction.

c09s04 Chapter 9: Corroboration is more than a matching story.
Converging evidence: support from different relevant methods. Publication bias: selective visibility of findings according to results. Researcher flexibility: choices in analysis that can affect conclusions.

Comparative, developmental, historical and experimental evidence can each address parts of a claim. Their limitations should be stated rather than counted as automatic confirmation.

A modern correlation may fit several histories. Seek independent predictions and consider how selective publication or flexible analysis could shape the visible literature.

c09s05 Chapter 9: What does the evidence allow?
Psychological research has documented substantial sampling and replication concerns. These affect evolutionary and non-evolutionary hypotheses alike.

Evolution’s general validity does not establish every specific psychological adaptation claim, and one weak hypothesis does not invalidate evolution.

c10s03 Chapter 10: Natural does not mean good or inevitable.
Is–ought gap: descriptive claims alone do not establish moral conclusions. Biological determinism: treating biology as fixing an unavoidable outcome. Moral reasoning: evaluating actions using ethical considerations.

A historical explanation cannot by itself justify an action. Nor does evolutionary influence imply that behaviour cannot change through learning or institutions.

Ethical judgments require reasons about welfare, rights, fairness and other values. Science can inform likely consequences without choosing those values on its own.

c10s03b Chapter 10: Use a bounded evolutionary account.
Hypothesis scope: conditions and outcomes a claim addresses. Alternative model: another explanation with distinct assumptions. Revision: changing confidence or account in response to evidence.

Specify the behaviour, level of explanation, proposed history and distinguishing predictions. Include development, culture and alternative evolutionary processes.

A good account admits where evidence is unavailable. Do not replace missing historical data with certainty based on modern intuition.

c10s04 Chapter 10: Practical decisions need current evidence.
Intervention evidence: data on the effect of a proposed change. Individual variation: differences within a population. Agency: capacity for meaningful choice and action.

A hypothesis about cooperation may suggest a practical question, but changing a policy needs evidence about current outcomes and harms. Preserve individual rights and choice.

Use this course to read research more carefully, not to assign hidden motives, rank groups or justify discrimination. An unfamiliar final case tests reasoning rather than professional authority.

c10s05 Chapter 10: What does the evidence allow?
Evolutionary psychology contains testable proposals, contested claims and methodological debates. Credibility depends on the evidence for each specific claim.

This course does not establish one ancestral lifestyle, fixed human roles or a policy agenda. Its final case is a self-check, not certification.
